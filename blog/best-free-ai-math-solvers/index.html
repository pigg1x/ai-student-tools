6 Best Free AI Math Solvers for Students (2026, Tested)

Math is the one subject where using an AI tool badly can genuinely set you back. In my first semester I used a photo solver to get through a problem set, understood almost none of it, and then sat in an exam staring at the same question type with nothing to fall back on. That is the mistake this page exists to prevent.

So I tested the six tools in our math and science category against real work: a calculus problem set, a linear algebra assignment and a physics sheet. What I was actually measuring was not whether they produce the right answer - most of them do. It was how much of the working they give away for free, because the working is the only part that teaches you anything.

If you want the short version: use Photomath or Symbolab to get unstuck, Desmos to understand, and Wolfram Alpha to check the answer you already worked out.

Wolfram Alpha

The most accurate of the group and the one I use least for learning. It computes symbolically rather than predicting plausible text, so when it tells you an integral or a determinant, you can treat the number itself as reliable.

The catch is the free tier. You get the answer to almost anything - derivatives, limits, matrices, systems of equations, unit conversions, even chemistry - but the full step-by-step solution is a paid feature, and the free hints often just restate the question in slightly different words. My routine with it is strict: work the problem on paper first, then type it in to check. When my answer disagrees with it, that is the moment I actually learn something, because I go and find which line I dropped a sign on.

Photomath

The one you have seen classmates point at a textbook. Photograph the question and you get a worked solution back in seconds.

On arithmetic through single-variable calculus it is fast and accurate, and importantly the free version shows steps rather than only the result - which is more than several of its competitors offer at the same price. Where it struggles is multi-step proofs, heavy notation, and anything where the method matters more than the number.

One habit worth building: check the transcription before you trust the output. It reads handwriting better than I expected, but not perfectly, and a misread minus sign gives you a completely confident wrong answer that looks just as convincing as a right one.

Symbolab

This is what I reach for with typed equations, and it is the best free option here for step-by-step algebra and calculus. Solutions are broken into named steps with the rule used at each one - apply the chain rule, factor the quadratic, substitute back - which is exactly what you need when your own notes have a gap in them.

Two things to know. Free accounts get a limited number of step-by-step solutions per day, and once you hit the cap you are staring at a subscription page that costs real money for a student. The advertising is also aggressive enough that I stopped opening it on my phone. When I do hit the limit mid-assignment, I photograph the working into my notes rather than wait for the counter to reset - mildly annoying, but it is what the cap forces you to do.

Mathway

Mathway covers the widest range of any free tool in this list - basic arithmetic up to calculus, plus statistics and chemistry - and it is the easiest to type a question into. The problem is what you get back: the final answer, with the step-by-step explanation locked behind a subscription.

That makes it a checking tool rather than a learning tool, and there are better free checkers. I keep it for one narrow job, which is quick sanity checks on the topics the others handle awkwardly, like statistics and unit conversion. If you are only going to install one app and you need working, this is not the one.

Desmos

Completely free, no account, and genuinely one of the best things on the internet for learning maths. Type a function and you see it. Drag a slider and the graph responds. Plot a table of data and fit a curve in seconds.

That interactivity is the point: it is the fastest way to build intuition for what a derivative, an asymptote or a transformation actually does, which is precisely what is missing when all you can do is shuffle symbols on paper. When a concept refused to stick for me, seeing it move usually fixed it faster than another worked example.

One practical detail: Desmos' graphing calculator is the one embedded in several digital exam platforms, so time spent learning it is also exam preparation. If I had to recommend a single tool from this page to a student who is struggling, it would be this one.

GeoGebra

GeoGebra does everything Desmos does and then keeps going - dynamic geometry, 3D plotting, spreadsheets, statistics and calculus in one free package. If your course involves constructions, vectors, solids of revolution or probability distributions, this replaces software your department may not even licence.

The trade-off is the interface, which is denser and takes an afternoon to feel natural. Spend that afternoon. I used it through a linear algebra module to visualise what a matrix does to a plane, and it did more for my understanding of eigenvalues than three weeks of lectures managed. Everything, including the mobile apps, is free, and there is nothing to unsubscribe from later.

The pattern is worth noticing: the tools that give away the reasoning for free are the ones with advertising or usage caps, and the tools with no limits are the ones that only hand you a number. Every free option charges you in either attention or completeness.

How to use them without hurting yourself

Three rules, all learned the hard way.

  • Attempt it first, tool second. Write your own solution before you look at theirs, even if it is wrong. A wrong attempt makes the explanation land completely differently from a blank page.
  • Never copy a step you cannot reproduce. If I cannot do the next question in the set unaided, I did not learn it, however good the working looked. That was my first-semester mistake and it cost me an exam.
  • Treat a surprising step as a warning. Automated working occasionally applies a rule that does not hold, or quietly changes a sign or drops a condition. If a step feels too convenient, redo it by hand.

One more thing, since it is the trap I fell into: keep the paper trail. When a solver unblocks me, I copy the method into my own notes in my own words, because the version inside the app is available to me in the exam hall exactly never.

Should you use these for graded assignments?

Check the policy before the problem set, not after. Most courses are fine with you using a tool to check your own work, but an increasing number restrict AI tools on graded work outright, and some examinations ban any device. Being able to show your own working and explain each step is the defence that holds up in both cases - which is, conveniently, the same thing that makes these tools useful in the first place.

If maths is not your only problem this term, the AI writing tools here handle the essay side, and if you are weighing up which chatbot to ask for explanations, I compared the three big ones on real coursework. Everything else I use sits in the full math and science category.
